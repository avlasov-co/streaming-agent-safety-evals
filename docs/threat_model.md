# Threat Model

This document outlines the threat model for the streaming agent safety benchmark.  A clear threat model helps clarify what kinds of unsafe behaviour the benchmark is designed to detect and what is out of scope.

## System boundary

The benchmark models a sequential decision system that observes a stream of market‑like events and must decide whether to take an action (`ACT_POSITIVE` or `ACT_NEGATIVE`) or abstain (`ABSTAIN`).  The system does not place real trades; actions are abstractions of making a directional bet.  Observations include predictions from a base model, a confidence score, volatility, latency, liquidity, spread, order imbalance, drift and a monitor risk score.  The system does not control the data generation process and cannot alter the underlying environment.

## Failure modes

The benchmark is designed to reveal the following classes of failures:

* **Overconfident incorrect actions**: the agent acts with high confidence even when the underlying prediction is wrong, leading to unsafe actions.
* **Acting under unsafe conditions**: the agent chooses to act when volatility is high, latency is extreme, liquidity is low or spreads are wide.  These conditions increase the risk of large losses or market impact.
* **Monitor failure**: the monitoring layer (represented by `monitor_risk_score`) fails to block a risky action due to imperfect risk assessment.
* **Excessive autonomy under uncertainty**: the agent continues to act in unfamiliar or shifted regimes instead of abstaining or requesting oversight.
* **Unsafe behaviour after distribution shift**: the agent exhibits safe behaviour in normal conditions but fails dramatically when the data distribution changes (adversarial shift, volatility spike, latency spike or liquidity crash).

## Assumptions

Several simplifying assumptions underlie the benchmark:

1. **Synthetic data**: The events are generated by a simple simulator rather than a real trading engine.  While the regimes capture general market features (volatility, latency, liquidity), they are stylised.
2. **Static policies**: The agents use fixed thresholds and do not adapt to feedback or learn over time.  There is no reinforcement learning, meta‑learning or tuning in deployment.
3. **No adversarial agent**: Although we include an adversarial regime, there is no explicit adversary controlling the environment.  Adversarial shift refers to a statistical change in the relationship between confidence and correctness.
4. **No oversight action**: Agents can abstain but cannot ask for human supervision or route decisions to a safer fallback.  Future versions will include an explicit `ASK_OVERSIGHT` action.

## Out of scope

The benchmark intentionally excludes the following:

* **Real financial trading**: It does not place real orders or incorporate realistic market microstructure beyond simplified features.
* **Model training and calibration**: The benchmark does not train models or calibrate confidence scores; it assumes predictions and confidence come from an upstream model.
* **Reward maximisation**: There is no profit or loss objective.  The purpose is to study unsafe actions, not to maximise returns.
* **Deployment recommendations**: The benchmark does not certify agents for real deployment or make recommendations about acceptable risk thresholds.

## Goals

The threat model guides the design of regimes, metrics and agents so that researchers can:

1. Detect when an agent is overconfident and unsafe under distribution shift.
2. Evaluate whether gating and monitoring reduce unsafe behaviour.
3. Understand the tradeoff between abstention (safety) and coverage (usefulness).
4. Identify gaps where an agent should defer to oversight or adapt to a new regime.

By articulating the system boundary and failure modes, this threat model provides context for interpreting benchmark results and motivates future extensions such as oversight actions, drift detection and multi‑step episodes.
